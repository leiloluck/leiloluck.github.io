Every bell strike cut out of bowls long interval.mp3 (55:16). The track is a sequence of recorded strikes separated by digital silence, so clips were cut at those silences — no filtering or editing. Then the set was cleaned: 15 clips contained overlapping strikes and were deleted; of the remaining 102 clips, repeated placements of the same recording were merged, keeping the longest tail; finally two same-pitch pairs turned out to be separate takes of the same bowl (97–99.8 % waveform-identical) and only one of each was kept. Reading the notes: "C3 −27¢" means the strongest partial sits 27 cents below standard C3 — a cent is 1/100 of a semitone (1200 per octave). Hand-made bowls are essentially never exactly on a note; that slight detune and the slow beating it causes are a large part of the character.

What the six bells are made of (measured)

Each bell is a sum of a few very stable partials. Two "families" appear — two different bowls, each recorded at three pitches. The lower table is from the literature on singing bowls (see resources/sources.md).

The extracted bells match this pattern remarkably well — including the beating pairs (1.3–5.8 Hz) and the 1 : 2.84 : 5.48 stack. That is what makes synthesis feasible: the recipe is only ~10 numbers per bell.

A · Generate from the pattern

A bell is a sum of damped sine waves — each partial gets a frequency, level, decay time and phase — plus a strike transient: a short noise burst split into low/mid/high bands, each decaying at its own rate, and every partial starts in phase the way a real strike excites it (modes alone or a naive noise click alone both sound synthetic — see sources.md, DAFx 2019). Randomize within the measured ranges and every bell is new but still belongs to the family. This runs in the page on the fly (no files); the whole recipe is the loop below.

The whole synthesis (it is this short) 
// one bell = sum of damped sines; partials taken from the family table above
const d = new Float32Array(len);            // len ≈ 15 s · 44100
for (const p of partials) {                 // ~6–12 partials
  const w = 2*Math.PI*p.f/sr;
  const d1 = Math.exp(-1/(p.tau*sr));                 // decay factor
  const d2 = Math.exp(-(1/p.tau + 1/p.rise)/sr);      // rise factor (contact time)
  let e1 = p.amp, e2 = p.amp, phase = p.phi;
  for (let i = 0; i < len; i++, e1 *= d1, e2 *= d2) {
    d[i] += (e1 - e2) * Math.sin(phase);   // rise then decay, like a struck resonator
    phase += w;                            // (+ a few cents of settling glide)
  }
}
// strike: one short noise burst split low/mid/high, each band decaying at its own
// rate (impact-sound recipe, sources.md). All modes start in phase: one polarity.
// Then normalize.

B · Manipulate the real recordings

The safest variation: replay an existing bell at a slightly different speed (playbackRate). Pitch and decay scale together, exactly as if the bowl were a bit smaller or bigger — artifact-free, but it can't leave the neighbourhood of the original: ±25–50¢ sounds like a sibling, ±100¢+ starts to sound like the same bell transposed. Layering two or three real bells a few tens of cents apart gives a richer composite that is still 100 % real recordings.

So how realistic is it?

For a meditation interval bell, both routes are realistic — the sustained ring is the easy part, because these bowls are almost pure steady partials (damping 0.002–0.015 %). Route A reproduces that faithfully as long as the beating pairs and the family-specific attack (sharp vs slow swell) are kept; it gives unlimited, never-repeating bells but the strike transient is the hardest part to fake. Route B is bulletproof and instantly convincing but only varies around six existing sounds.

Suggested next step (for the app, later): keep the six real bells and pick one at random per interval with a tiny random repitch (±10–20¢) — no repetition, no risk. That route is developed in the Tuned tab: live pitching measurements, loudness-matched chords, and interval patterns. Synthesized bells (route A) can be auditioned as extra material and, if they hold up against the real ones by ear, mixed in as additional voices.

1 · Live pitching vs. a pre-rendered library

You chose route B (pitch the real bells). Should it happen live on the device, or should pitched files be baked into a small library? Measured on this machine — Chrome, six bells, real files:

live — playbackRate on decoded bellspre-rendered library
one-time costdecode all six: ~0.5 s of CPU total, 40 MB RAMzero on the device (paid at build time)
per pitched bell~0.01 ms — a rate parameter; the audio engine re-samples natively for free~30–70 ms render + encode per variant, plus a file to ship
files to shipthe six bells, 1.75 MB total6 bells × N variants; 5 variants ≈ 8 MB, 12 ≈ 20 MB
timingsample-accurate scheduling on the audio clock (chords, patterns, lock screen)fine per file, but arbitrary chord combinations can't be baked without a combinatorial explosion
flexibilityany detune value, any combination, instantlyonly the baked variants (which you'd re-pitch with playbackRate anyway)

Verdict: pitch live. Re-sampling is native to the audio engine — the only real costs are a one-time decode (~40 MB RAM for six bells; can be lazy or mono if a phone ever complains) and exactly nothing per note. A pre-rendered library buys nothing here, except the option of time-preserving pitch shift — which is precisely the manipulation we do not want: route B's charm is that a pitched bell is pitched and stretched together (a smaller/larger bowl). Pre-render only if you ever need a single shareable stream file.

Baking moves work to build time but buys nothing on the device — resampling is native, chords can't be baked combinatorially, and every baked file adds download weight and a generation of lossy encoding.

2 · Chords — 2–3 bells at once, same loudness as one bell

Why the old layer test was "quite loud and off": the three bells launched as separate media elements (tens of ms apart, sometimes more), and three bells simply stacked sum to about +4.8 dB. The fix is a gain system — measured loudness (stereo RMS) of the six bells:

Their loudness spans 6.3 dB — the quietest and loudest differ by more than a doubling of amplitude. Note: the flat family is recorded almost out of phase between channels (correlation −0.67) while the tuned family is in phase (+0.66) — a mono downmix loses up to 6 dB on the flat ones, so loudness must be measured in stereo, which is what headphones and stereo speakers play.

The system: normalise every bell to one reference loudness (here the quietest, so no gain exceeds 1), then a chord of N bells divides each member by √N (−3 dB for two, −4.8 dB for three). Uncorrelated sounds add in power, so dividing amplitudes by √N gives the sum the power of one bell: a chord rings as loud as a single bell. Measured result: the tuned trio lands 1.3 dB under a single bell (residual comes from the bells being slightly correlated). Every event in a session — single or chord, at any pitch — can then sit at one session loudness; that is what makes 30 minutes of random bells feel calm instead of jumpy.

The chord onset — the first tens of milliseconds — is a separate problem with its own measurements and options: § 3. Chords themselves are out of the final product; this stays for the record.

3 · The chord onset — why the first moments feel off, and the options

Feedback: chords sound good during the sound, but a bit odd at the very start. Three measured contributors:

contributorwhat was measured
1 · An unreliable head on every bell fileThe six bells are stream slices of the source MP3 (copied, not re-encoded). A slice starts mid-frame, so its first ~10–25 ms is reconstructed differently by different decoders — for the C3 bell the same file's first 20 ms comes out 22 dB apart (ffmpeg vs libmpg123). A single bell hides this; a baked chord triples it: the trio carries ≈ −17 dBFS rms of sound in its first 25 ms, before the strike proper.
2 · The strike transients bunchThree strikes of the same instruments, recorded the same way, land within a few ms of each other — the attack transients add almost linearly, while the sustained partials add as power (the ÷√N law of § 2). The trio's onset peak therefore sits ≈ 6 dB above the scaled peak of its loudest single member — hotter than the balanced body around it.
3 · DensityC3+G3+C4 stacks three sharp attacks. The duet (chord 03) has two; the flat set's soft swell is too slow to bunch at all — its trio shows no onset spike.

The three fixes below all start from the same trio and can be combined. Re-cut cures 1 at the source; stagger and soft onset cure 2 — and in the live app both are free: a scheduling offset or a gain envelope on one buffer source, nothing to bake.

4 · When should the next bell come? (interval distributions)

First, what the long track you extracted from actually does: 140 strikes over 55 min, median gap 24.5 s (IQR 18.8–25.5 s) — a near-metronome at ~24 s that never waits for the bells to ring out (the big C3 bell rings 28 s) and sometimes fires tight repeats at 8–15 s. That regularity — not the sounds — is what made it feel repetitive.

Left: interval distribution. Right: a simulated 10-minute session with the ring drawn as a bar — nothing overlaps, by construction.

Common rule for all schemes: an event's wait clock starts once the previous bell is barely audible — the moment it crosses a set threshold (default −40 dB below its own peak; adjustable in the player below). The bell itself is never cut: it keeps ringing underneath the wait and even underneath whatever comes next. Measured on these six recordings, waiting for true silence instead is pointless — from −40 dB to the noise floor takes under a second — the part that actually feels long is the quiet-but-audible stretch above the threshold, which is now visible and tunable. A chord counts as one event.

schemewhat it feels like
Fixed (every 60 s)what the app did before. Perfectly predictable; pleasant for minutes, mechanical over half an hour.
Uniform [45–90 s]bounded and safe, but flat: every wait in the range is equally likely, so it neither surprises nor breathes. Reads as "random", not as natural.
Gaussian 65 ± 15 s (truncated 45–90)most gaps cluster near the average → the ear learns the typical wait and it feels regular again; the tail never produces a genuinely long pause.
Truncated exponential — wait ~Exp counted from the barely-audible point; floor 8 s; default cut-off at twice the longest sound — no gap beyond 2 × 28 s = 56 s, with the cut mass re-spread over the shorter waits (adjustable in the player)Recommended. This is the memoryless distribution of a Poisson process: the wait doesn't know how long you've already waited, so it is mathematically unpredictable — usually a few seconds, occasionally right up to the cut-off. The floor guarantees no overlap; the cut-off puts a hard ceiling on the longest gap and the probability of anything beyond it is exactly zero — the remaining mass is redistributed smoothly over the shorter waits (re-normalised density, no spike at the limit). (People systematically misperceive true randomness — real streaks feel non-random — which is why the tail should be capped; see sources.md.)

Crackle test — original vs. safe version (A/B)

The crackle is intermittent and never at the same spot, which rules out a corrupted file (that would click at the same moment every time) and points at the playback link itself: Chromium's Bluetooth audio buffering, or the speaker waking from standby. This section isolates what remains. Each bell is playable two ways: original, and a safe version pre-rendered with a 5 ms fade-in (removes any pop at the very first sample) and a lookahead limiter pinned at −1 dBFS. The safe files are lossless FLAC and their measured peaks are identical to the originals to six decimals — nothing here even reaches −2.3 dBFS, so the limiter does nothing audible. That is the point: if you can hear any difference at all, tell me. Also new: every play now waits 0.4 s after the click, and the link-warm option below keeps the audio stream from ever going quiet.

Denoise test — original vs. strength menu

Every bell plays its original plus spectral subtraction of increasing strength (all lossless FLAC). The number on each cell is that file's measured hiss-band reduction; ring level moves 0.01 dB at most and the mallet strike loses 1.2–2.9 dB. FFT strong (nr24) and FFT max (nr40) are the lighter settings, good on all bells. FFT ultra was rebuilt this round to fix the wub-wub: that underwater modulation came from the denoiser's gains wobbling frame to frame, so ultra now runs the deep subtraction with slow gain adaptation — same depth (59–62 dB gone, including the C3's stubborn 4–8 kHz layer that nothing else touched) with a measurably steadier residual, and barely touching tonal content on C3/C4 (~2 dB). On the G3 −27¢ ultra still shaves one partial ~29 dB — ear-check it, and take FFT max there if it bothers. Deeper than this, subtraction starts eating the bell itself: that is the practical ceiling. (Tried and not shown: NLM — too subtle by ear; DeepFilterNet3 — deleted the partials outright; hand-built spectral gates — ate the bell or did nothing; dry/wet blending — re-imports the noise. Details in resources/sources.md.)

Tuned 3 · the palette, recut: three bells, seven pitches each

Two bells leave the palette: G3 −27¢ (bell_003.mp3 — audible ring to −40 dB: 14.0 s) and C4 (bell_006.mp3 — 13.8 s). They were the two shortest tails of the five-bell set — the others ring 18.7–28.0 s — and by ear they stop noticeably early, as if the source had damped them. The flat C4 −27¢ (bell_005, 8.3 s) already sits on the shelf from the previous round for the same reason.

In exchange the pitch window widens: every remaining bell now slides −3 … +3 semitones (was −2…+2; the C4's −4…0 retires with the bell). 3 bells × 7 pitches = 21 palette entries. A lower strike is automatically a longer one — live playbackRate stretches pitch and time together, so −3 rings ~19 % longer and +3 ~16 % shorter, exactly like a slightly larger or smaller bowl. That's the natural relation, not an artefact; nothing needs compensating. The palette's shortest audible ring rises from 13.8 s to 15.7 s (the G3 at +3), and its span becomes 108–233 Hz — one semitone lower than the old palette ever went at the bottom (108.3 vs 114.7 Hz), two semitones below the retired C4's ceiling at the top.
