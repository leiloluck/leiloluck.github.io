### PsychonautWiki — Benzodiazepines — https://psychonautwiki.org/wiki/Benzodiazepines — accessed 2026-07-24

Benzodiazepines are a class of oral CNS depressants acting as positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A. The page provides an **equivalent-dose table** benchmarked to 10 mg diazepam: alprazolam 0.5 mg, clonazepam 0.5 mg, lorazepam 1 mg, etizolam 1 mg — noting equivalents "differ from those used by other authors" and "may vary between individuals." Half-lives: alprazolam 6–12 h, lorazepam 10–20 h, clonazepam 18–50 h, etizolam 4–12 h, diazepam 20–100 h (36–200 h with active metabolite). Short-acting agents are used for insomnia/acute panic; long-acting for anxiety disorders. **Dangerous interactions:** "Fatal overdose may occur when benzodiazepines are combined with other depressants such as opiates, barbiturates, gabapentinoids, thienodiazepines, alcohol or other GABAergic substances" — the combination potentiates muscle relaxation, amnesia, sedation and respiratory depression, and at higher doses causes "sudden, unexpected loss of consciousness along with a dangerous amount of depressed respiration." Dissociatives unpredictably potentiate amnesia/sedation/motor-control loss; stimulants mask the sedative effect, producing intensified disinhibition and dangerous black-out states. **Tolerance** to sedative-hypnotic effects develops within a couple of days of continuous use and returns to baseline in 7–14 days; benzos are "extremely physically and psychologically addictive." **Withdrawal:** sudden discontinuation "can cause seizures (which may be life-threatening)"; tapering is advised; risks include high blood pressure, seizures and death. Amnesia is characteristic; "delusions of sobriety" (false belief one is sober despite severe impairment) occur at heavy doses.

### PsychonautWiki — Diazepam (dosing & duration) — https://psychonautwiki.org/wiki/Diazepam — accessed 2026-07-24

Oral dosage (diazepam): **Threshold 1 mg; Light 2.5–5 mg; Common 5–15 mg; Strong 15–30 mg; Heavy 30 mg+.** Duration (oral): **Total 4–8 hours; Onset 20–40 minutes; Peak 60–90 minutes; After-effects 12–36 hours.** The page does not separately list come-up or come-down phases. These diazepam values are used as the site's diazepam-equivalent reference tiers; the long after-effect tail reflects diazepam's long elimination half-life and active metabolites, making it a representative *long-acting* benzodiazepine (short-acting benzos such as alprazolam or etizolam produce a shorter, sharper curve).

### TripSit — Benzodiazepines factsheet — https://wiki.tripsit.me/wiki/Benzodiazepines — accessed 2026-07-24

TripSit's factsheet uses diazepam (Valium) as the reference standard for its dosage/potency table (e.g. alprazolam 0.5 mg, lorazepam 1 mg ≈ 10 mg diazepam) and notes wide duration variation — short-acting triazolam ~2 h half-life versus diazepam 20–100 h (36–200 h with active metabolites). The factsheet's central safety message: **"An extremely high percentage of drug-related deaths are due to mixing benzos with other drugs, especially opiates and alcohol,"** and **"Withdrawals can be fatal."** Listed negative effects include high addiction potential, memory loss, and risk of blackout. It advises avoiding driving and operating machinery, and warns benzodiazepines act as a CNS depressant that is dangerous when mixed with other drugs. The machine-readable `combos.json` (key `benzodiazepines`) rates alcohol, GHB/GBL, opioids and tramadol as **Dangerous**; dissociatives (ketamine, DXM, PCP, MXE) as Caution/Unsafe; and stimulants as "Low Risk & Decrease" but with the caveat that stimulants mask sedation so overdose can follow when the stimulant wears off first.

### Talk to Frank — Benzodiazepines — https://www.talktofrank.com/drug/benzodiazepines — accessed 2026-07-24

Frank distinguishes prescription benzodiazepines from illicit "street benzos," which "can be far more dangerous" and are "unpredictable in content and in potency" — tablets sold as Valium or Xanax "frequently contain other potent benzodiazepines or dangerous chemicals instead," and specific concerning imprints named include DAN 5620, T-20 and MSJ. Effects: sleepy, relaxed, calm, confused/disoriented, slower reflexes; onset "around 30 mins," lasting "around 1–4 hours depending on type." **Critical dangers:** "It is dangerous to take benzodiazepines with alcohol and/or other drugs" — combining with alcohol, heroin, opioids, pregabalin or gabapentin "increases the risk of overdose and death" through CNS/respiratory depression. Counterfeit street benzos are "causing people to be hospitalised" and deaths. Crushing and injecting is "extremely dangerous" (abscesses, rapid fatal overdose). Benzodiazepines are "highly addictive"; heavy-user withdrawal includes tremors, nausea, vomiting, headaches, anxiety, panic attacks, depression and — in some — seizures. **Harm-reduction steps:** never use alone; start with half a pill and wait one hour before taking more; avoid unknown sources.

### Ashton Manual — Benzodiazepine equivalence table (benzo.org.uk) — https://www.benzo.org.uk/bzequiv.htm — accessed 2026-07-24

Professor Heather Ashton's widely cited manual gives approximate doses equivalent to **10 mg oral diazepam**: alprazolam 0.5 mg, clonazepam 0.5 mg, lorazepam 1 mg (also chlordiazepoxide 25 mg, oxazepam 20 mg, temazepam 20 mg, nitrazepam 10 mg, among others). The table underscores that short-acting benzodiazepines (alprazolam, lorazepam, oxazepam) are considerably **more potent by weight** than long-acting ones (diazepam, clonazepam, chlordiazepoxide), and that all equivalents are estimates that vary between individuals and reference sources and should be used only as approximate conversions. This is the clinical basis for expressing the guide's dose tiers in diazepam-equivalent milligrams and for the ~20× potency warning (0.5 mg alprazolam ≈ 10 mg diazepam). Corroborated by NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service and GlobalRPH benzodiazepine converters, which give the same core equivalences.

### Correlation / TEDI — European Drug Checking Trends 2018–2024 — https://correlation-net.org/2025/01/08/european-drug-checking-trends-2018-2024/ — accessed 2026-07-24

The Trans-European Drug Information (TEDI) network's review flags a critical 2024 emerging trend: **metonitazene (a nitazene opioid) being substituted for benzodiazepines in tablets.** The report states novel nitazene opioids "are largely confined to the benzodiazepine sedative market at present," meaning users expecting a sedative can instead receive a potent synthetic opioid. TEDI data manager Guy Jones: "The risk of combining opioids and benzos and the problems associated with misselling mean that services need to be able to deal with this trend." The report stresses that "unexpected strength and combinations of tablets increase risk significantly" for benzodiazepine users, since missold products may contain opioids rather than the expected benzo. It also notes European drug-checking coverage is uneven — roughly half of all samples come from just two services (Spain and the UK) — so prevalence figures are partial. This is the direct evidentiary basis for the guide's counterfeit-pill and nitazene warnings.

### EUDA — New psychoactive substances (European Drug Report 2025) — https://www.euda.europa.eu/publications/european-drug-report/2025/new-psychoactive-substances_en — accessed 2026-07-24

The EU Drugs Agency reports that counterfeit/falsified medicines containing potent synthetic opioids have surged across Europe — seizures of falsified tablets rose from ~380 (2022) to over 50,000 (2024), with at least 12 countries reporting to the EU Early Warning System in 2024. These fakes most often mimic oxycodone ("OC/80," "M30") and, to a lesser extent, **benzodiazepines**. A documented Danish case involved counterfeit green "M30" tablets containing a mixture of protonitazene, protonitazepyne and the **designer benzodiazepine bromazolam**. Designer-benzo patterns are shifting (e.g. phenazolam, metabolite-only detections). **Nitazenes** are laboratory-made opioids that can be far more potent than heroin, so even small amounts carry a risk of life-threatening poisoning — and a naloxone dose may be outlasted by the drug. The practical harm-reduction implication: the imprint, colour and even blister packaging of a "Xanax"/"Valium"/oxycodone tablet are no guarantee of authenticity, so drug-check where possible and start with a fraction.
