### PsychonautWiki — Nitrous Oxide — https://psychonautwiki.org/wiki/Nitrous_Oxide — accessed 2026-07-24

Dosing is given in grams and cream-charger cartridges (inhaled): threshold 4 g; light 4–8 g (½–1 cartridge); common 8–16 g (1–2); strong 16–40 g (2–5); heavy 40 g+ (>5). The exact toxic dose is unknown. Onset is 5–10 seconds, come-up 5–10 seconds, peak 15–30 seconds, offset 1–5 minutes, total 1–5 minutes, after-effects 15–30 minutes. Tolerance builds with repeated use, halves after 3–7 days, returns to baseline in 1–2 weeks. Long-term toxicity centres on vitamin B12: "frequent and/or chronic use can cause vitamin B12 depletion" because "nitrous temporarily inhibits methionine synthase, a B12-dependent enzyme," producing numbness and tingling in fingers, toes and lips, progressing to numbness of all extremities; users are advised to supplement B12 regardless of dose/frequency, with vegans, vegetarians and people with malabsorption at particular risk. Acute dangers: improper use risks oxygen deprivation — medical N₂O is 50% nitrous / 50% oxygen, whereas whipped-cream chargers contain no oxygen, enabling hypoxia and asphyxiation; very cold gas taken directly from a tank can freeze the lips and throat (frostbite); loss of gross/fine motor control, balance and coordination is prevalent, so users should sit down before onset; plastic dispensers have burst and, rarely, killed. Dangerous combinations: alcohol, GHB/GBL, opioids and tramadol all potentiate ataxia and sedation and can cause unexpected loss of consciousness, with vomit-aspiration risk — use the recovery position.

### TripSit — Nitrous (factsheet & combination chart) — https://drugs.tripsit.me/nitrous — accessed 2026-07-24

TripSit's machine-readable combination data (mirrored in the guide's `tripsit-combo/combos.json` under key `nitrous`) rates the depressant pairings as **Caution**: alcohol, GHB/GBL, opioids and tramadol each carry the note that "both substances potentiate the ataxia and sedation caused by the other and can lead to unexpected loss of consciousness at high doses. While unconscious, vomit aspiration is a risk if not placed in the recovery position. Memory blackouts are likely." Pregabalin is also **Caution**, with an added, well-referenced warning that combining may increase seizure risk (and citing asphyxial-death and case-report literature on recreational nitrous). Benzodiazepines are "Low Risk & Decrease." Notably, most classic recreational drugs — cannabis, cocaine, amphetamines, ketamine, MDMA, LSD, mushrooms, mescaline, DMT, MXE, 2C-x, PCP — are listed as "Low Risk & Synergy," i.e. TripSit does not flag them as dangerous with nitrous (though additive incoordination with other dissociatives like ketamine is clinically sensible to respect). Factsheet-level guidance stresses that chargers contain no oxygen, that improper use risks oxygen deprivation, that B12 supplementation is advised for any user, and that anyone who loses consciousness should be placed in the recovery position and monitored for breathing.

### Talk to Frank — Nitrous oxide (laughing gas) — https://www.talktofrank.com/drug/nitrous-oxide — accessed 2026-07-24

Describes the method: people open the canister, transfer the gas into a container (usually a balloon), then inhale from the balloon; direct inhalation from canisters is "particularly dangerous because the gas is under such high pressure and it comes out as extremely cold." Effects (euphoria, relaxation, calmness, fits of giggles) "last 1–2 mins on average," and because it is short-acting it "can lead people to frequently re-dose." Key risks: "if you take too much nitrous oxide you risk falling unconscious and/or suffocating from the lack of oxygen. People have died this way." On chronic harm: "heavy regular use of nitrous oxide can lead to a deficiency of vitamin B12 and to a form of anaemia. Severe B12 deficiency can lead to serious nerve damage, causing tingling and numbness in the fingers and toes." Nerve damage "can be very painful and make walking difficult. It can even lead to paralysis, and the damage may be lasting." Impaired white blood cell formation is also noted. Safer-use advice: "never place a plastic bag over your head," avoid direct canister inhalation and enclosed spaces, and recognise it is hard to judge a safe amount. Mixing drugs is always risky; driving while impaired is dangerous and illegal. Legally it is now a UK Class C drug (possession up to 2 years; supply up to 14 years).

### Crew (Scotland) — Nitrous oxide — https://www.crew.scot/drug/nitrous-oxide/ — accessed 2026-07-24

Practical harm-reduction guidance. Method: gas from cartridges is released into party balloons using a "cracker," "charger" or dispenser, and "it is essential that the gas is not inhaled directly from a cartridge, as the gas is cold and under high pressure, and this can damage the tissue of the mouth, throat and lungs." Effects come on fast and are short-lived. Health risks: "excessive and prolonged use of nitrous oxide is linked to nerve damage which can affect motor control and coordination," and nitrous "can lead to a depletion of vitamin D and B12," with symptoms including "pins and needles in hands/feet, weakness, tiredness, stumbling, confusion and problems with memory." Concrete safer-use tips: "sit down when inhaling the balloon — you could collapse if taking the balloon quickly while standing"; "avoid breathing in and out of the balloon as this will reduce your oxygen levels"; avoid "chain balloons" and take breaks between use; "if using a mask to inhale nitrous avoid strapping it to your face — if you pass out, this can cause suffocation and death"; and avoid enclosed spaces such as cars or tents. This source is the basis for the "sit down," "one balloon at a time," "don't rebreathe," "no mask," and "no enclosed spaces/cars" items in the entry.

### EUDA/EMCDDA — Recreational use of nitrous oxide: a growing concern for Europe / European Drug Report 2024 (Other drugs) — https://www.euda.europa.eu/system/files/media/publications/documents/14934/20225054_PDF_TD0922561ENN_002.pdf — accessed 2026-07-24

The European Union Drugs Agency reports rising recreational nitrous use among young people; the drug is "widely available, cheap and popular," and discarded canisters have become a visible drug-litter issue in several countries. It links nitrous to "poisonings, burns and lung injuries and, in some cases of prolonged exposure, neurotoxicity from vitamin B12 deficiency." EUDA notes there is genuine "debate on the extent to which this substance is associated with negative health risks, especially among episodic users," while stressing that its growing popularity makes it an important area for monitoring, and that "some patterns of use [are] likely to increase the risk of adverse consequences" — in particular the move from small 8 g cream chargers toward larger catering-size cylinders/tanks, which enable much larger volumes to be consumed. This is the institutional European source behind the entry's "prefer small chargers over large tanks" and "limit total volume" framing, and it corroborates the B12-neurotoxicity headline harm at population level. ### Neurologic and Thrombotic Complications in the Setting of Chronic Nitrous Oxide Abuse (PMC9859688) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9859688/ — accessed 2026-07-24

Clinical case series establishing the mechanism and severity of nitrous harm. "Nitrous oxide inactivates vitamin B12 through oxidation of the cobalt moiety," causing "indirect irreversible inhibition of methionine synthase, which… converts homocysteine to methionine" — i.e. a functional B12 deficiency independent of dietary intake. Resulting neurological effects include "numbness, weakness, ataxia, polyneuropathy, bone marrow suppression, and megaloblastic anemia." One patient developed bilateral foot numbness, urinary retention, ataxia with frequent falls, and proximal lower-limb weakness (3/5 strength); labs showed megaloblastic anaemia with hypersegmented neutrophils — the picture of subacute combined degeneration. The accumulated homocysteine also produced a hypercoagulable state: deep vein thromboses (popliteal, tibial, peroneal) and extensive bilateral pulmonary emboli, with homocysteine "greater than four times the upper limit of normal." Harm was tied to heavy patterns — one patient used whippets daily for two years, "using multiple canisters in a day." Importantly for prognosis, recovery was significant with treatment: after stopping nitrous and receiving supplemental B12, the patient regained normal gait and strength at two-month follow-up with B12 normalisation — underscoring that B12 plus cessation is the treatment, that early detection matters, and that continued heavy use risks lasting damage. This source supports the entry's B12/methionine-synthase mechanism, the SACD/neuropathy symptom list, the DVT/PE risk, and the "watch for tingling/numbness/unsteady gait — see a doctor early" items.
